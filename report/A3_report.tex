\section*{Executive Summary}
\noindent\textbf{The problem.} Text-to-speech turns written text into spoken audio. It powers screen readers for people with low vision, audiobook and e-learning narration, announcements and customer-service voices. Commercial systems are built by large teams on large computers; we asked how good a voice a small team can build \emph{from scratch} with a laptop and free cloud computing, and which design choices matter most.

\noindent\textbf{What we delivered.} A complete system that takes any English sentence, including numbers, dates, money and common abbreviations, and produces a recording in the voice of one speaker. One command (\texttt{scripts/synth.py}) turns text into an audio file. The voice was learned from about 19 hours of a single speaker reading books aloud.

\noindent\textbf{What we compared.} The part of the system that ``paints'' the sound can be built in two ways: one predicts it directly in a single step; the other generates it gradually in ten steps, which in principle gives more lifelike detail. We built both, each in a large and a small size, and trained all four on identical data for identical time. We then repeated everything with less than a third of the recordings, to see how much the amount of data matters.

\noindent\textbf{What we found.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The voice is highly intelligible.} An automatic speech recogniser misheard 2.8--4.8\% of our words, against 1.6\% on the speaker's real recordings.
  \item \textbf{More data was the biggest win.} With 19 instead of 5.5 hours of recordings, recognition errors fell by a factor of two to three, and the robotic, even rhythm of the smaller models largely disappeared.
  \item \textbf{The simpler single-step design was slightly clearer}, not worse as we expected (2.8\% vs 4.0\% errors), and the small versions were as good as the large ones.
  \item \textbf{It is fast and cheap to run.} A sentence takes about 0.2 seconds on a laptop's graphics chip and 0.5 seconds on its ordinary processor, i.e.\ 7 to 25 times faster than real time, with no special hardware.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Feasibility and scaling.} Training one voice took under an hour on a free cloud GPU. When serving many users, the bottleneck is the final ``waveform'' stage (over 80\% of the run time), not our model; it can be served in parallel on ordinary servers. The largest quality gains will come from more recordings of the target speaker, then from a larger model.

\noindent\textbf{Recommendation.} Use the single-step design in its small size (\expD: 4.9 million parameters, as intelligible as the largest model) for real-time and low-cost deployment, and collect more speaker data before investing in a bigger model. \ph{confirm or adjust after the group's blind listening test}

\section{Introduction}
Neural text-to-speech (TTS) is usually built in three stages \citep{shen2018tacotron2,ren2021fastspeech2}: a \emph{front-end} turns text into phonemes, an \emph{acoustic model} predicts a mel spectrogram, and a \emph{vocoder} turns the spectrogram into a waveform. The acoustic model decides what is said, for how long and with which spectral detail, so it carries most design choices. A central one is how its final mel \emph{decoder} is trained. \emph{Regression} decoders \citep{ren2021fastspeech2} predict the mel directly with an $L_1$/$L_2$ loss: fast and simple, but known to over-smooth, because they learn the average of all plausible renditions. \emph{Generative} decoders, such as diffusion \citep{popov2021gradtts} or conditional flow matching \citep{mehta2024matcha}, learn a distribution and sample from it, at the cost of several network passes.

\noindent\textbf{Aim and scope.} Build a complete, reproducible single-speaker English TTS system on LJSpeech \citep{ito2017ljspeech}, with an acoustic model implemented and trained from scratch and a pretrained, frozen HiFi-GAN vocoder \citep{kong2020hifigan}; and measure under controlled conditions how three variables affect intelligibility, quality and speed. Multi-speaker training, style control and vocoder training are out of scope.

\noindent\textbf{Experimental plan.} A $2\times2$ factorial design, \emph{decoder objective} (flow matching vs regression) $\times$ \emph{decoder depth} (8 vs 4 residual blocks), trained on the full training split; the same four models are repeated on a 3{,}000-utterance subset as a third controlled variable, \emph{data size}. This gives three research questions:
\textbf{RQ1} Does flow matching give better speech than regression of the same size, and at what inference cost?
\textbf{RQ2} How much does decoder depth matter, and does the answer depend on the objective?
\textbf{RQ3} How much does training-data size matter, and does it affect both objectives equally?

\noindent\textbf{What ``good'' means here.} (i) \emph{Intelligibility}: the words are recognisable (ASR word/character error rate); (ii) \emph{fidelity}: the spectrum and rhythm are close to the real speaker (mel-cepstral distortion, duration ratio); (iii) \emph{naturalness}: the speech does not sound robotic (listening, spectrograms); (iv) \emph{speed}: faster than real time on ordinary hardware (real-time factor, latency). Section~\ref{sec:metrics} justifies each metric.

\section{Dataset and Protocol}\label{sec:data}
\textbf{Source and format.} LJSpeech~1.1 \citep{ito2017ljspeech}: 13{,}100 audio--text pairs (23.92\,h) of one female speaker reading non-fiction, 22{,}050\,Hz mono 16-bit WAV, with \texttt{metadata.csv} holding an ID, the raw transcript and a normalised transcript. All files were checked for format, readability and a matching transcript (nb §1.6). The metadata must be read with \texttt{QUOTE\_NONE}: with default CSV quoting, quote characters swallow the delimiter and 16 transcripts appear empty.

\noindent\textbf{Split.} 80/10/10 = 10{,}480 / 1{,}310 / 1{,}310 clips: the IDs are sorted, shuffled once with \texttt{numpy} seed 42 and cut at 80\% and 90\%. The ID lists are saved with an MD5 fingerprint (\texttt{78fda0eda5bbdcaecb57fd47e9b20610}) that is stored in every config and checkpoint, so all runs provably share the split. \textbf{Training} uses the full training split (about 19\,h after trimming) for the main runs, and for RQ3 a \textbf{3{,}000-utterance subset} (5.5\,h): the first 3{,}000 IDs of a seed-42 permutation of the training split, within the task sheet's 1--3k guidance. \textbf{Validation:} a fixed 300-clip sample of the validation split is scored every epoch and selects \texttt{best.ckpt}. \textbf{Test:} never used for training or selection; Section~\ref{sec:results} reports a seeded 100-sentence sample \ph{replace with all 1,310}. Vocabulary and mel statistics come from each run's own training data only, so nothing leaks from validation or test.

\noindent Table~\ref{tab:modules} gives each module's role. The \textbf{training loss} is the sum of a duration loss (MSE on log-durations from Monotonic Alignment Search, MAS), a prior loss (Gaussian negative log-likelihood of the target mel under $\mu_y$) and the decoder loss. The \textbf{flow-matching} decoder \citep{lipman2023flowmatching,mehta2024matcha} samples $x_0\!\sim\!\mathcal N(0,I)$ and $t\!\sim\!U[0,1]$, forms $x_t=(1-(1-\sigma_{\min})t)\,x_0+t\,x_1$ and regresses its velocity $v_\theta(x_t,\mu_y,t)$ onto $x_1-(1-\sigma_{\min})x_0$; at inference it integrates from noise (temperature 0.667) with 10 Euler steps. The \textbf{regression} decoder is the same network without the time input, trained with masked $L_1$ to output $x_1$ in one pass. Only the objective differs, so RQ1 is a clean comparison.

\section{Controlled Experiments}\label{sec:expdesign}
\subsection{Experiment design}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Objective} (\expA\ vs \expC, \expB\ vs \expD). \emph{Why:} the defining choice between FastSpeech-style and Matcha-style models. \textbf{H1:} regression over-smooths, so it is less natural and scores worse on quality metrics. \textbf{H2:} regression is faster (1 decoder pass vs 10).
  \item \textbf{Depth} (\expA\ vs \expB, \expC\ vs \expD). \emph{Why:} model size drives cost on small devices. \textbf{H3:} 4 blocks are faster with a small quality loss, larger for flow matching, which must model a whole distribution.
  \item \textbf{Data size} (each experiment, 3{,}000 vs 10{,}480 utterances). \emph{Why:} data is the cost that scales a voice. \textbf{H4:} more data mainly improves the duration predictor and hence rhythm; regression, which over-fitted earliest on the subset, gains most. With a fixed epoch budget the full-data runs also take 3.5$\times$ more updates, so data size and update count change together.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Shared settings:} Adam (lr $10^{-4}$), batch 32, gradient clipping 1.0, seed 42, 300 epochs (327 steps per epoch on full data, 93 on the subset). \texttt{best.ckpt} is the epoch with the lowest validation loss within each run. Total losses are not comparable across objectives (velocity MSE vs mel $L_1$), so runs are compared only through the duration and prior losses, which come from identical modules, and through the test-set metrics.

\noindent\textbf{RQ1, objective.} Regression was \emph{more} intelligible than flow matching at equal depth (Table~\ref{tab:compare}: $-1.2$ and $-1.7$ points, both CIs exclude 0), at both data sizes. H1 is therefore \emph{rejected for intelligibility}. Two observations explain it. First, flow matching \emph{samples}: noise at temperature 0.667 adds variability that occasionally blurs a phoneme, while regression outputs the clear, averaged spectrum that ASR favours. Second, the step sweep (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}) shows that fewer Euler steps, which keep the sample closer to the mean, \emph{lower} \expA's WER (4.3\% at 1 step vs 5.7\% at 10). The flow decoder's expected advantage is naturalness, which WER does not measure; MCD \ph{interpret: MCD for C vs A} and the listening notes (Section~\ref{sec:qual}) address it. H2 holds for the acoustic model, regression being 4.5$\times$ faster on the GPU and 5$\times$ on the CPU, but \emph{not end to end}: HiFi-GAN takes 81--99\% of synthesis time, so all four run at RTF 0.04 on the GPU and 0.13--0.14 on the CPU, 7--25$\times$ faster than real time.

\noindent\textbf{RQ2, depth.} Halving the decoder (\expA$\rightarrow$\expB: $-1.84$\,M parameters; \expC$\rightarrow$\expD: $-1.58$\,M) changed WER by under one point, and neither CI excludes 0. The loss was larger for flow matching (+0.85 vs +0.30), the direction H3 predicts, but not significantly. Validation losses agree: within each objective the two depths differ by under 0.01. The 4-block models are 1.5--2$\times$ faster in the acoustic model, which again barely affects end-to-end speed.

\noindent\textbf{RQ3, data size.} Data size had by far the largest effect: 3.5$\times$ more data cut WER by 9.4 and 8.9 points for flow matching (about 3.3$\times$) and by 3.6 and 4.5 points for regression (about 2.3$\times$), all CIs far from 0. The duration predictor confirms H4's mechanism: on the subset it over-fitted (\expA: training duration loss 0.41 vs validation 0.55); on full data the gap shrank (0.38 vs 0.44) and the speaking rate moved closer to the speaker's (duration ratio 0.86--0.92 $\rightarrow$ 0.91--0.92). The regression runs over-fitted the subset early (best epochs 78 and 67, then rising validation loss) but not the full split (best epochs 224 and 174). Contrary to H4, however, \emph{flow matching} gained most: learning a distribution needs more data than learning its mean, so the generative decoder is the more data-hungry one.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{System design justification}
The pipeline follows the Glow-TTS/Matcha-TTS family because each constraint pointed to it (Table~\ref{tab:justify}): phonemes compensate for a small single-speaker corpus; MAS removes the need for an external aligner and makes alignment failures impossible; a non-autoregressive model is fast enough for real time on a CPU; and a frozen, matched HiFi-GAN delivers high-fidelity audio without days of vocoder training. The evidence supports the choice: the vocoder costs only 0.1 WER points over the real recording, alignments never skipped words, and a full training run fits in under an hour on a free GPU. The cost of the compact 1-D convolutional decoder (instead of Matcha's U-Net with Transformer blocks) is capacity, which is the most likely reason the flow decoder did not out-perform regression (Section~\ref{sec:errors}).

\subsection{Error analysis}\label{sec:errors}
We inspected the sentences with the highest WER and listened to the prompts. Of 100 test sentences, 41 were transcribed perfectly for all four full-data models and 16 contained an error for all four, which points to causes shared by every model, in the front-end and the duration predictor, rather than the decoder.

\noindent\textbf{Failure 1: front-end errors on abbreviations, initials and rare names.} \emph{Evidence:} \textit{``Baker St.''} is normalised to \textit{``baker saint''} (prompt 06); the initials in \textit{``O.\,H.\ Lee''} keep their full stops and are read with sentence-final pauses, transcribed \textit{``OH, Leon''} (\expA) and \textit{``OH, Lee''} (\expC); rare names are mispronounced by espeak-ng (\textit{Esagil} $\rightarrow$ \textit{Esajil}, \textit{De Mohrenschildt} $\rightarrow$ \textit{De Moren-Skeltt}). \emph{Cause:} the abbreviation rules are context-free, initials are not recognised as such, and the grapheme-to-phoneme rules have never seen the names. The model can only say the phonemes it is given. \emph{Fix:} context rules (\textit{St.} after a capitalised word $\rightarrow$ \textit{street}; before a name $\rightarrow$ \textit{saint}), spelling out single capital letters followed by a full stop (\textit{O.\,H.} $\rightarrow$ \textit{oh aitch}) without a pause, and a small pronunciation lexicon for names that occur in LJSpeech.

\noindent\textbf{Failure 2: rushed speech that blurs word boundaries.} \emph{Evidence:} every model speaks 8--9\% faster than the speaker (duration ratio 0.91--0.92; 0.86 for the 3k regression models), and the typical errors merge or split words at boundaries: \textit{``not idle''} $\rightarrow$ \textit{``nut isle''}, \textit{``Forrester, after''} $\rightarrow$ \textit{``forced her after''}. \emph{Cause:} the duration predictor regresses \emph{log}-durations with MSE, which estimates the geometric mean of each phoneme's duration, below its arithmetic mean; short, unstressed phonemes and pauses at commas lose the most. The shortfall was larger on the subset, where durations over-fitted. \emph{Fix:} at inference, a global \texttt{length\_scale} of about 1.09 (one line, no retraining); in training, a loss on linear durations or a stochastic duration predictor \citep{kim2021vits}, and explicit pause tokens at punctuation.

\noindent\textbf{Failure 3 (flow models): occasional slurred phonemes from sampling.} \emph{Evidence:} flow matching has higher WER than regression at both sizes, and fewer sampling steps reduce it (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}). \emph{Cause:} sampling noise (temperature 0.667) through a small decoder that has not fully learned the distribution. \emph{Fix:} lower temperature (0.3--0.5) for intelligibility-critical use, and a higher-capacity decoder (Matcha's U-Net with Transformer blocks) trained on more data, which Section~\ref{sec:results} shows the flow decoder needs most.

\subsection{Trade-off analysis}
\textbf{Quality vs speed.} For this system the trade-off sits almost entirely in the vocoder: 10 flow-matching steps cost 0.03--0.05\,s per sentence against 0.15\,s (GPU) to 0.5\,s (CPU) for HiFi-GAN. Regression or fewer Euler steps buy little end-to-end speed (RTF 0.043 $\rightarrow$ 0.038 on the GPU), while the step sweep shows the flow model's quality--speed knob is nearly flat from 2 to 20 steps. Real speed gains therefore require a lighter vocoder (HiFi-GAN V2/V3), not a cheaper decoder. \textbf{Training data and time vs quality.} The largest measured trade-off is data: 3.5$\times$ more data, and 3.5$\times$ more updates, cut errors 2.3--3.3$\times$, while training time per run was under one hour on a GPU (vs 3--4.5\,h for the 3k runs on a laptop). Doubling the decoder's depth, by contrast, cost 1.6--1.8\,M parameters for a non-significant gain. \textbf{Data efficiency vs ceiling.} Regression is the safer choice when data is scarce (WER 6.4\% vs 13.4\% on the subset); flow matching closes most of the gap with more data and is the variant expected to benefit from larger models.

\section{Conclusion and Recommendations}
We built a complete text-to-speech system with a from-scratch, Matcha-style acoustic model and a pretrained HiFi-GAN vocoder, and compared four decoders at two data sizes under controlled conditions. \textbf{RQ1:} regression was slightly but significantly more intelligible than flow matching (WER 2.84\% vs 3.99\%), and 5$\times$ faster in the acoustic model, which hardly matters end to end. \textbf{RQ2:} 4 decoder blocks were as good as 8 within measurement error. \textbf{RQ3:} training data dominated: 3.5$\times$ more data cut errors 2.3--3.3$\times$, most for flow matching.

\noindent\textbf{Recommendation.} Deploy \expD\ (regression, 4 blocks, 4.85\,M parameters): it is within 0.3 WER points of the best model and the cheapest to run, at 0.17\,s per sentence on a laptop GPU and 0.54\,s on a CPU. Prefer \expA\ only if listening tests show a clear naturalness advantage \ph{confirm with listening results}.

\noindent\textbf{Deployment and scaling.} (i) \emph{What breaks first} is vocoder throughput: HiFi-GAN takes over 80\% of the compute, so under load it should be batched across requests on a GPU, or replaced by a lighter generator on CPU-only servers. (ii) \emph{Latency} for long texts: synthesise sentence by sentence and stream, since a sentence returns in 0.2--0.5\,s. (iii) \emph{Quality} scales with data first (RQ3), then model capacity; a new voice needs 10--20\,h of clean recordings, or a multi-speaker model with a speaker embedding fine-tuned on less. (iv) \emph{Languages:} espeak-ng covers about 100 languages, but the normaliser rules are English-specific. (v) Training cost is modest: under 1\,GPU-hour per voice at this size.

\noindent\textbf{Future improvements.} Context-aware normalisation and a name lexicon (Failure 1); duration correction and pause tokens (Failure 2); a U-Net/Transformer flow decoder with lower sampling temperature (Failure 3); a predicted-MOS metric (e.g.\ UTMOS) and a formal listening test; several seeds per configuration.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations.} One speaker and one seed per configuration (run-to-run variance unmeasured); test results on a 100-sentence sample \ph{full test split}; automatic metrics in place of a formal MOS test; data size and update count change together in RQ3, and the two sizes were trained on different hardware, so their training times are not comparable; the vocoder was trained on all of LJSpeech, including our test clips, which makes the vocoded upper bound optimistic but affects all systems equally.
