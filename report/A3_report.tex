\section*{Executive Summary}
\noindent\textbf{The problem.} Text-to-speech turns written text into spoken audio. It powers screen readers for people with low vision, audiobook and e-learning narration, announcements and customer-service voices. Commercial systems are built by large teams on large computers; we asked how good a voice a small team can build \emph{from scratch} with free cloud computing, and which design choices matter most.

\noindent\textbf{What we delivered.} A complete system that takes any English sentence, including numbers, dates, money and common abbreviations, and produces a recording in the voice of one speaker. One command (\texttt{scripts/synth.py}) turns text into an audio file. The voice was learned from about 19 hours of a single speaker reading books aloud.

\noindent\textbf{What we compared.} The part of the system that ``paints'' the sound can be built in two ways: one predicts it directly in a single step; the other generates it gradually from random noise over several steps, which in principle gives more lifelike detail. We built both, each in a large and a small size, and trained all four on identical data for identical time. For the step-by-step design we also measured how the number of steps trades quality against speed.

\noindent\textbf{What we found} (1{,}310 test sentences the models never saw).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The voice is highly intelligible.} An automatic speech recogniser misheard 2.7--5.4\% of our words, against 2.1\% on the speaker's real recordings.
  \item \textbf{The simpler single-step design was clearer}, not worse as we expected: 2.7\% against 5.0\% misheard words at the same size, and closer to the real voice on a standard spectral measure.
  \item \textbf{The larger models were slightly better} (by about 0.3--0.4 points), a small but reliable difference.
  \item \textbf{Clearer is not the same as more natural.} In our listening notes the step-by-step design sounded the most natural (3.7 out of 5 against 3.3), while the single-step design was the clearest and had the best rhythm.
  \item \textbf{Fewer generation steps were clearer, not worse.} Cutting the step-by-step design from 10 steps to 1 lowered misheard words from 5.0\% to 3.7\%, which also makes it cheaper to run, but it still did not reach the single-step design (2.7\%).
  \item \textbf{It is fast and cheap to run.} A sentence takes 15--60 milliseconds on a cloud GPU and about half a second on an ordinary processor, i.e.\ 7--9 times faster than real time even without a GPU.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Feasibility and scaling.} Training one voice took 42--56 minutes on a free cloud GPU. On ordinary processors the bottleneck is the final ``waveform'' stage (78--97\% of the run time), not our model; it can be run in parallel on standard servers. The next quality gains will come from better handling of names and abbreviations, and from a larger model trained on more recordings of the target speaker.

\noindent\textbf{Recommendation.} Use the single-step design in its larger size (\expC: the most intelligible model, and as fast as the small one on a processor) for real-time and low-cost deployment. Where a natural-sounding voice matters more than every word being perfect, and a GPU is available, the step-by-step design (\expA) is the alternative.

\section{Introduction}
Neural text-to-speech (TTS) is usually built in three stages \citep{shen2018tacotron2,ren2021fastspeech2}: a \emph{front-end} turns text into phonemes, an \emph{acoustic model} predicts a mel spectrogram, and a \emph{vocoder} turns the spectrogram into a waveform. The acoustic model decides what is said, for how long and with which spectral detail, so it carries most design choices. A central one is how its final mel \emph{decoder} is trained. \emph{Regression} decoders \citep{ren2021fastspeech2} predict the mel directly with an $L_1$/$L_2$ loss: fast and simple, but known to over-smooth, because they learn the average of all plausible renditions. \emph{Generative} decoders, such as diffusion \citep{popov2021gradtts} or conditional flow matching \citep{mehta2024matcha}, learn a distribution and sample from it, at the cost of several network passes.

\noindent\textbf{Aim and scope.} Build a complete, reproducible single-speaker English TTS system on LJSpeech \citep{ito2017ljspeech}, with an acoustic model implemented and trained from scratch and a pretrained, frozen HiFi-GAN vocoder \citep{kong2020hifigan}; and measure under controlled conditions how the decoder's design affects intelligibility, quality and speed. Multi-speaker training, style control and vocoder training are out of scope.

\noindent\textbf{Experimental plan.} A $2\times2$ factorial design, \emph{decoder objective} (flow matching vs regression) $\times$ \emph{decoder depth} (8 vs 4 residual blocks), all trained on the full training split; and, for the flow-matching baseline, a sweep over the number of sampling steps at inference. This gives three research questions:
\textbf{RQ1} Does flow matching give better speech than regression of the same size, and at what inference cost?
\textbf{RQ2} How much does decoder depth matter, and does the answer depend on the objective?
\textbf{RQ3} How does the number of ODE sampling steps trade quality against speed for the flow-matching decoder?

\noindent\textbf{What ``good'' means here.} (i) \emph{Intelligibility}: the words are recognisable (ASR word/character error rate); (ii) \emph{fidelity}: the spectrum and rhythm are close to the real speaker (mel-cepstral distortion, duration ratio); (iii) \emph{naturalness}: the speech does not sound robotic (listening, spectrograms); (iv) \emph{speed}: faster than real time on ordinary hardware (real-time factor, latency). Section~\ref{sec:metrics} justifies each metric.

\section{Dataset and Protocol}\label{sec:data}
\textbf{Source and format.} LJSpeech~1.1 \citep{ito2017ljspeech}: 13{,}100 audio--text pairs (23.92\,h) of one female speaker reading non-fiction, 22{,}050\,Hz mono 16-bit WAV, with \texttt{metadata.csv} holding an ID, the raw transcript and a normalised transcript. All files were checked for format, readability and a matching transcript (nb §1.6). The metadata must be read with \texttt{QUOTE\_NONE}: with default CSV quoting, quote characters swallow the delimiter and 16 transcripts appear empty.

\noindent\textbf{Split.} 80/10/10 = 10{,}480 / 1{,}310 / 1{,}310 clips: the IDs are sorted, shuffled once with \texttt{numpy} seed 42 and cut at 80\% and 90\%. The ID lists are saved with an MD5 fingerprint (\texttt{78fda0eda5bbdcaecb57fd47e9b20610}) that is stored in every config and checkpoint, so all runs provably share the split. \textbf{Training} uses the whole training split (about 19\,h). \textbf{Validation:} a fixed 300-clip sample of the validation split is scored every epoch and selects \texttt{best.ckpt}; afterwards every saved checkpoint was re-scored on all 1{,}310 validation clips to check that the sample was representative (Section~\ref{sec:results}). \textbf{Test:} all 1{,}310 clips, never used for training or selection. Vocabulary and mel statistics come from the training split only, so nothing leaks from validation or test.

\noindent\textbf{Preprocessing.} Every step was decided from a measurement of the raw data (nb §1.7, Figure~\ref{fig:raw}) and checked afterwards (nb §1.11). Table~\ref{tab:preproc} lists each decision with its evidence.

\noindent Table~\ref{tab:modules} gives each module's role. The \textbf{training loss} is the sum of a duration loss (MSE on log-durations from Monotonic Alignment Search, MAS), a prior loss (Gaussian negative log-likelihood of the target mel under $\mu_y$) and the decoder loss. The \textbf{flow-matching} decoder \citep{lipman2023flowmatching,mehta2024matcha} samples $x_0\!\sim\!\mathcal N(0,I)$ and $t\!\sim\!U[0,1]$, forms $x_t=(1-(1-\sigma_{\min})t)\,x_0+t\,x_1$ and regresses its velocity $v_\theta(x_t,\mu_y,t)$ onto $x_1-(1-\sigma_{\min})x_0$; at inference it integrates from noise (temperature 0.667) with a fixed number of Euler steps, 10 by default. The \textbf{regression} decoder is the same network without the time input, trained with masked $L_1$ to output $x_1$ in one pass. Only the objective differs, so RQ1 is a clean comparison.

\section{Controlled Experiments}\label{sec:expdesign}
\subsection{Experiment design}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Objective} (\expA\ vs \expC, \expB\ vs \expD). \emph{Why:} the defining choice between FastSpeech-style and Matcha-style models. \textbf{H1:} regression over-smooths, so it is less natural and scores worse on quality metrics. \textbf{H2:} regression is faster (1 decoder pass vs 10).
  \item \textbf{Depth} (\expA\ vs \expB, \expC\ vs \expD). \emph{Why:} model size drives cost on small devices. \textbf{H3:} 4 blocks are faster with a small quality loss, larger for flow matching, which must model a whole distribution.
  \item \textbf{Sampling steps} (\expA\ at 1, 2, 4, 10 and 20 Euler steps; same weights, same seeded noise per sentence). \emph{Why:} the number of steps is the one knob only a generative decoder has, and it sets its inference cost. \textbf{H4:} quality improves with more steps and saturates at a few steps, since OT-CFM learns nearly straight paths \citep{lipman2023flowmatching,mehta2024matcha}, while the acoustic model's run time grows linearly with the number of steps.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Shared settings:} Adam (lr $10^{-4}$), batch 32, gradient clipping 1.0, seed 42, 300 epochs of 327 steps each on the full training split. \texttt{best.ckpt} is the epoch with the lowest validation loss within each run. Total losses are not comparable across objectives (velocity MSE vs mel $L_1$), so runs are compared only through the duration and prior losses, which come from identical modules, and through the test-set metrics. Table~\ref{tab:settings} summarises the four runs.

\section{Evaluation and Results}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Metrics and protocol}\label{sec:metrics}

Synthesis noise is seeded per sentence, so all flow models see the same noise. Uncertainty is a 95\% bootstrap interval over sentences (1{,}000 resamples); differences use a \emph{paired} bootstrap on the same resampled sentences.

\noindent\textbf{RQ1, objective.} Regression was \emph{more} intelligible than flow matching at equal depth, by 2.3 and 2.4 WER points, and closer to the recording in MCD, by 0.34 and 0.42\,dB; all four intervals exclude 0 (Table~\ref{tab:compare}). H1 is therefore \emph{rejected} on both automatic measures. Two observations explain it. First, flow matching \emph{samples}: noise at temperature 0.667 adds variability that occasionally blurs a phoneme (only 53--55\% of sentences were transcribed perfectly, against 70--72\% for regression), while regression outputs the clear, averaged spectrum that both ASR and MCD favour. Second, our decoder is a compact 1-D convolutional network, much smaller than Matcha's U-Net with Transformer blocks, so it may not have the capacity to model the whole distribution well. The flow decoder's expected advantage is naturalness, which neither metric measures; the listening notes (Section~\ref{sec:qual}) address it.

\noindent\textbf{RQ2, depth.} Halving the decoder (\expA$\rightarrow$\expB: $-1.84$\,M parameters; \expC$\rightarrow$\expD: $-1.58$\,M) cost a small but reliable amount of quality: $+0.43$ and $+0.31$ WER points and $+0.16$ and $+0.08$\,dB MCD, all intervals excluding 0 (Table~\ref{tab:compare}). As H3 predicted, the loss was larger for flow matching, about twice as large in MCD. The 4-block models are 1.2--1.7$\times$ faster in the acoustic model, which matters on the GPU (60 $\rightarrow$ 40\,ms for flow matching) but not on the CPU, where the vocoder dominates (0.481\,s for both regression models).

\noindent\textbf{RQ3, sampling steps.} Quality did not improve with more steps; intelligibility got steadily \emph{worse}. WER rose from 3.65\% at 1 step to 5.02\% at the default 10 and 5.31\% at 20 steps, and every step count differs reliably from 10 (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}): 1 step is 1.37 points better ($[-1.68, -1.07]$), 20 steps 0.28 points worse. MCD has a shallow minimum at 4 steps (6.72\,dB) and varies by only 0.15\,dB in total. H4 is therefore \emph{rejected}: there is no saturation point, because the extra steps do not add intelligibility at all. The explanation is the same as for RQ1. One Euler step from noise moves the sample straight to the decoder's prediction of the \emph{average} target given the text, which is essentially what the regression decoder outputs; each further step adds sampled detail and noise-driven variation, which a listener may hear as more natural but which blurs phonemes for ASR. Even at 1 step, flow matching stays behind regression (3.65\% vs 2.67\% for \expC, non-overlapping intervals), so the gap in RQ1 is not only a matter of the step count. On the M4 the end-to-end RTF changed little (0.041 at 2 steps, 0.048 at 20) because the vocoder dominates; the 1-step timings were disturbed by other work on the laptop early in the run (medians of successive fifths 0.044--0.076, against a stable spread of about 0.002 for the other step counts) and should be ignored. On the T4, where the ten decoder passes take 50 of \expA's 60\,ms (Table~\ref{tab:speed}), cutting the steps would matter much more. Table~\ref{tab:sweep} and Figure~\ref{fig:sweep} give the numbers, and the right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:qs} places them next to regression.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{System design justification}
The pipeline follows the Glow-TTS/Matcha-TTS family because each constraint pointed to it (Table~\ref{tab:justify}): phonemes compensate for a single-speaker corpus of about 10k training sentences; MAS removes the need for an external aligner and makes alignment failures impossible; a non-autoregressive model is fast enough for real time on a CPU; and a frozen, matched HiFi-GAN delivers high-fidelity audio without days of vocoder training. The evidence supports the choice: the vocoder adds no measurable error over the real recording, the alignment is monotonic by construction, so the classic attention failures of skipping and repeating words cannot occur, and a full training run fits in under an hour on a free GPU. The cost of the compact 1-D convolutional decoder (instead of Matcha's U-Net with Transformer blocks) is capacity, which is the most likely reason the flow decoder did not out-perform regression (Section~\ref{sec:errors}).

\subsection{Error analysis}\label{sec:errors}
We inspected the sentences with the highest WER and listened to the prompts. Of the 1{,}310 test sentences, 491 (37\%) were transcribed perfectly for all four models and 261 (20\%) contained an error for all four, which points to causes shared by every model, in the front-end and the duration predictor, rather than the decoder. Errors are also concentrated in short sentences: WER on sentences of up to 10 words is 4.5--8.2\%, against 2.1--4.3\% on sentences of more than 20 words, because short LJSpeech clips are often fragments full of names.

\noindent\textbf{Failure 1: front-end errors on abbreviations, initials and rare names.} \emph{Evidence:} \textit{``Baker St.''} is normalised to \textit{``baker saint''}, and \textit{lives} is mispronounced by every model (prompt 06); the initials in \textit{``O.\,H.\ Lee''} keep their full stops and are read with sentence-final pauses, transcribed \textit{``OH, Lee''} (\expC) and \textit{``L.H.\ Leon''} (\expA); rare names are mispronounced by espeak-ng (\textit{Esagil} $\rightarrow$ \textit{Essigil}, \textit{De Mohrenschildt} $\rightarrow$ \textit{De Moren-Skeltt}). \emph{Cause:} the abbreviation rules are context-free, initials are not recognised as such, and the grapheme-to-phoneme rules have never seen the names. The model can only say the phonemes it is given. \emph{Fix:} context rules (\textit{St.} after a capitalised word $\rightarrow$ \textit{street}; before a name $\rightarrow$ \textit{saint}), spelling out single capital letters followed by a full stop (\textit{O.\,H.} $\rightarrow$ \textit{oh aitch}) without a pause, and a small pronunciation lexicon for names that occur in LJSpeech.

\noindent\textbf{Failure 2: rushed speech that blurs word boundaries.} \emph{Evidence:} every model speaks 7--8\% faster than the speaker (duration ratio 0.917--0.931), and typical errors merge or split words at boundaries: \textit{``knew Lee Harvey''} $\rightarrow$ \textit{``newly Harvey''} (\expC), \textit{``Forrester, after''} $\rightarrow$ \textit{``forced her after''} (\expA). \emph{Cause:} the duration predictor regresses \emph{log}-durations with MSE, which estimates the geometric mean of each phoneme's duration, below its arithmetic mean; short, unstressed phonemes and pauses at commas lose the most. \emph{Fix:} at inference, a global \texttt{length\_scale} of about 1.08 (one line, no retraining); in training, a loss on linear durations or a stochastic duration predictor \citep{kim2021vits}, and explicit pause tokens at punctuation.

\noindent\textbf{Failure 3 (flow models): occasional slurred phonemes from sampling.} \emph{Evidence:} flow matching has a higher WER than regression at both depths and transcribes fewer sentences perfectly (53--55\% vs 70--72\%). The step sweep confirms that sampling is part of the cause: with 1 step, which removes most of the sampled variation, \expA's WER falls from 5.02\% to 3.65\% (RQ3), but it stays above regression. \emph{Cause:} sampling noise (temperature 0.667) through a small decoder that has not fully learned the distribution. \emph{Fix:} a lower temperature (0.3--0.5) for intelligibility-critical use, 1--2 sampling steps (RQ3), and a higher-capacity decoder (Matcha's U-Net with Transformer blocks).

\subsection{Trade-off analysis}
\textbf{Quality vs speed.} On a CPU the trade-off sits almost entirely in the vocoder: the acoustic model costs 13--134\,ms per sentence against about 460\,ms for HiFi-GAN, so a cheaper decoder buys little (0.59 $\rightarrow$ 0.48\,s). On a GPU the balance flips: the ten flow-matching passes take 50 of \expA's 60\,ms, so the decoder objective, its depth and the number of sampling steps (RQ3) all matter there. Real speed gains on CPUs therefore require a lighter vocoder (HiFi-GAN V2/V3), not a cheaper decoder. \textbf{Model size vs quality.} Doubling the decoder's depth costs 1.6--1.8\,M parameters and about 13\,min more training per run, for a small but reliable gain (0.3--0.4 WER points), and no extra latency on a CPU. \textbf{Generative vs regression.} At this model size, regression wins on every automatic measure and on speed; the case for flow matching rests on naturalness and on scaling to larger decoders, which we could not afford to test. \textbf{Steps vs quality.} The step count is not the usual quality--speed knob here: fewer steps were both faster and clearer (RQ3), so the trade-off it controls is intelligibility against sampled detail, which only a listening test at each step count can price.

\section{Conclusion and Recommendations}
We built a complete text-to-speech system with a from-scratch, Matcha-style acoustic model and a pretrained HiFi-GAN vocoder, and compared four decoders under controlled conditions on all 1{,}310 test sentences. \textbf{RQ1:} regression was significantly more intelligible than flow matching (WER 2.67\% vs 4.95\% at 8 blocks) and closer to the recording in MCD, and 5--7$\times$ faster in the acoustic model, which matters on a GPU but hardly on a CPU. \textbf{RQ2:} 8 decoder blocks were slightly but reliably better than 4 (0.3--0.4 WER points), more so for flow matching. \textbf{RQ3:} fewer sampling steps made flow matching \emph{more} intelligible (WER 3.65\% at 1 step vs 5.02\% at 10), rejecting H4, but even then it did not reach regression; by ear (one listener) \expA\ at 10 steps was the most natural model.

\noindent\textbf{Recommendation.} Deploy \expC\ (regression, 8 blocks, 6.43\,M parameters): it is the best model on WER, CER and MCD, and on a CPU it is as fast as the smaller \expD\ (0.48\,s per sentence), because the vocoder dominates there. Choose \expD\ only where GPU latency or memory is the binding constraint (15 vs 18\,ms per sentence), and \expA\ where naturalness matters more than intelligibility and a GPU is available: it was rated the most natural model by ear (3.67 vs 3.25 out of 5), at the cost of about twice the WER of \expC.

\noindent\textbf{Deployment and scaling.} (i) \emph{What breaks first} is vocoder throughput on CPUs: HiFi-GAN takes 78--97\% of the compute there, so under load it should be batched across requests on a GPU, or replaced by a lighter generator on CPU-only servers. (ii) \emph{Latency} for long texts: synthesise sentence by sentence and stream, since a sentence returns in 15--60\,ms on a GPU and about 0.5\,s on a CPU. (iii) \emph{Quality} scales with the front-end first (Failure 1 affects every model), then model capacity and data; a new voice needs 10--20\,h of clean recordings, or a multi-speaker model with a speaker embedding fine-tuned on less. (iv) \emph{Languages:} espeak-ng covers about 100 languages, but the normaliser rules are English-specific. (v) Training cost is modest: under 1 GPU-hour per voice at this size.

\noindent\textbf{Future improvements.} Context-aware normalisation and a name lexicon (Failure 1); duration correction and pause tokens (Failure 2); a U-Net/Transformer flow decoder with a lower sampling temperature (Failure 3); a predicted-MOS metric (e.g.\ UTMOS) and a blind listening test with several listeners; several seeds per configuration.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations.} One speaker and one seed per configuration (run-to-run variance unmeasured); automatic metrics and one non-blind listener in place of a formal MOS test; the step sweep tests only the 8-block flow model, was timed on a laptop, and was not listened to at each step count; the vocoder was trained on all of LJSpeech, including our test clips, which makes the vocoded upper bound optimistic but affects all systems equally.
